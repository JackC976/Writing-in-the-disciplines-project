Introduction

Improvement and efficiency sit at the heart of manufacturing. Lean Six Sigma lets companies minimize errors, eliminate waste, and reduce costs by making data-based decisions and focusing on the customer’s needs (George et al., 2019).

Six Sigma

Sigma refers to a deviation from an average value, so six sigma means six standard deviations from the mean. At that level, 99.9999998% of manufactured parts fall within tolerance, which is close to perfection. The method assumes that process changes such as tool wear will push results off center, so it builds in a 1.5 sigma buffer. That buffer leads to the practical goal of 3.4 defects per million parts (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 142).

Six Sigma reaches that precision through the DMAIC standard, explained below. It takes existing methods of efficiency and gives them a path to improvement: analyze current conditions, make a matching change, then monitor that change over time.

Lean

Lean manufacturing comes from the Toyota Production System (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 138) and was later used alongside Six Sigma. Its goal is to reduce as much waste as possible while providing the highest value to the customer (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 139).

Lean directs employees to look at every process in a company and categorize it as either value adding or value subtracting. Common wastes include excess inventory, unnecessary motion, unnecessary transportation, and unnecessary waiting (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 139). Waste creates bottlenecks that starve parts of the process and add cost and time. For example, stocking large amounts of steel ties up funds that could have gone into development, and it takes up space. Once that is identified as a larger waste than other processes, action can be taken to cut inventory and free money and space for more profitable uses.

DMAIC, step by step

DMAIC stands for Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, and Control (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 142). The steps run in order, and the cycle repeats as a company works through its problems. Here is each step with the shaft example from the industry analysis.

  1. Define. Before anything can improve, the goal must be clear. Example: shaft X must have a cylindricity tolerance of 0.001 inches.
  2. Measure. Collect data on how the process performs today. That could mean automating inspection with machine learning or inspecting a sample from a batch.
  3. Analyze. If a problem keeps recurring, study the whole system to find where bottlenecks, damage, or other issues arise.
  4. Improve. When a substantial problem is found, act on it. Example: buy additional, more-precise lathes to make the shaft.
  5. Control. After the change, monitor it to see whether results improved. If they did, maintain the new process so the gains last.

Applied over time, this loop can make efficiency skyrocket, because every change is based on data and then checked against more data.

Lowering your DPMO with DMAIC

A high DPMO does not fix itself. A company lowers it by working through DMAIC:

  1. Define the target, such as the tolerance a part must meet, so you know what counts as a defect.
  2. Measure current performance. Counting units, opportunities, and defects, as this calculator does, gives the starting DPMO.
  3. Analyze where the defects come from. In the axle case, bearing size and dents caused most rejections (Guleria et al., 2022, p. 1741).
  4. Improve the process at those sources. That company bought a high-precision gauge and padded the axles during transport (Guleria et al., 2022, p. 1743).
  5. Control the gains by measuring again. Enter the new defect count here: a lower DPMO and a higher sigma level show the change worked, and a flat result means returning to Analyze.

History

Improvement and efficiency have been at the heart of manufacturing for as long as people have made things, whether rubber ducks or jet engine blades. Lean Six Sigma can trace its roots back to the early 20th century. Every step along the way shared one search: efficiency through analysis. Before any improvement, working conditions must be analyzed thoroughly and trends shown clearly enough to expose weaknesses in the process (Snee, 2004, p. 7).

A century of building blocks

  • Early 1900s: Frederick Taylor. Taylor determined that labor and planning needed to be separate categories. This laid the groundwork for the labor and management model that is still seen in virtually every company today (Snee, 2004, p. 5).
  • The control chart. It showed performance over time along with the changes made, so engineers could see what was working and what was not (Snee, 2004, p. 7).
  • Acceptance sampling. Instead of testing everything, this method took a sample from a large batch and tested only its contents (Snee, 2004, p. 7).
  • Exploratory Data Analysis. It made graphical analysis an important step in improvement, so information could be digested faster (Snee, 2004, p. 7).
  • Quality methodologies. These developments led to Statistical Quality Control (SQC), Statistical Process Control (SPC), and Total Quality Management (TQM) (Snee, 2004, p. 7).

Two roots, one method

Lean: Toyota, after World War II

A shortage of materials after the war forced Toyota to be as efficient as possible to survive, and the approach allowed the company to thrive. Over the following years that finding was developed into the Lean methodology, built around reducing waste (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 138).

Six Sigma: Motorola, 1980s

Engineer Bill Smith developed Six Sigma at Motorola (Clegg et al., 2010, p. 142). It brought the decades of analysis tools above into a single standard, DMAIC, aimed at near-perfect precision.

Bringing them together

By practicing Lean Six Sigma, a company can focus on identifying and fighting all waste. That frees up time and money to apply DMAIC through Six Sigma and improve products and efficiency. Combined, these practices help companies keep their place in a competitive market.

The pattern across a century: split planning from labor, track performance over time, test smartly, and always analyze before changing anything. Lean Six Sigma packages that pattern into one repeatable method.

Current Uses

Lean Six Sigma delivers results across countries and industries. The two case studies below each followed DMAIC, but they faced different problems: one was slow production, the other was defective parts.

Ecuadorian metalworking company

Campoverde-Pillajo et al. (2026) followed the DMAIC approach to solve bottleneck issues at a company that builds machines.

  1. Define. The time from the start to the end of construction was substantial.
  2. Measure. The average lead time for the company’s machines was 67 days.
  3. Analyze. After all processes were studied, the electrical and mechanical assemblies stood out as bottlenecks, caused by poor planning, materials management, and disorganization (p. 5).
  4. Improve. The company introduced checklists, gave employees further training, and optimized the work area. Lead time fell to 42 days.
  5. Control. The company added data charts, frequent audits, and process evaluations (p. 6).

The first pass took about three months (p. 11). Savings were projected at $17,300 per year over five years against an initial investment of $5,200 (p. 13).

Indian axle manufacturer

Guleria et al. (2022) applied Six Sigma’s DMAIC principles at an automobile axle manufacturer.

  1. Measure. The axle defect rate was over 10% per month, a considerable amount of product.
  2. Analyze. Bearing size and dents were the largest contributors to out-of-compliance axles (p. 1741).
  3. Improve. The company bought a high-precision measurement gauge that notifies the machinist when a part is at the correct tolerance. It also improved transportation by placing a padded material between axles.
  4. Control. The new practices were upheld. Lead time decreased from 11 to 10 days, and axle rejection dropped to slightly over 3% (p. 1743).

What the cases have in common

  • Analysis found the real cause. Both companies studied the whole process before acting, which pointed to specific assemblies in one case and to bearing size and dents in the other.
  • Fixes were practical. Checklists, training, a better layout, a precision gauge, and padding for transport are modest changes with measurable results.
  • Gains were locked in. Both cases ended with a Control step, such as audits, charts, or upheld practices.

Together they show that Lean Six Sigma can drive results globally and across many disciplines.

Why Lean Six Sigma

The problem

The rate of technological improvement is faster than ever. New technologies make manufacturing faster, more precise, and more customizable (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 261), but they do not implement themselves. A company needs a framework that can strategically bring in new technology and increase efficiency. Lean Six Sigma (Campoverde-Pillajo et al., 2026, p. 1) and Kaizen (Sahmi & El Abbadi, 2024, p. 169) both perform well, so the choice between them has to be made carefully.

What the solution must do

Three criteria are required:

  1. Improve efficiency over time (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 261).
  2. Adapt to change and continuously support the customer (Kumar et al., 2020, p. 90).
  3. Cost less to implement than the funds saved (Sichinsambwe et al., 2023, p. 5).

Two more are preferred: 4. minimize waste (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 261) and 5. minimize environmental impact (Leal Filho et al., 2025, p. 2). Each option was scored in a decision matrix, with required criteria out of 10 points and preferred criteria out of 5.

How each option scored

Lean Six Sigma

LSS stands out for its emphasis on data analysis (Snee, 2004, p. 7). It uses DMAIC to drive efficiency by reducing waste (Campoverde-Pillajo et al., 2026, p. 1), including intellectual, physical, and time waste (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 262). Repeating the cycle of fixing, analyzing new data, and fixing again produces real efficiency gains over time (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 261).

Criteria 1 and 3: 10 each. In the Ecuadorian case, lead time dropped from 67 to 42 days (p. 5) and savings were projected at $17,300 per year against a $5,200 investment (p. 13).

Criterion 2: 5. DMAIC takes significant time, which makes LSS less adaptable. The case study took three months (p. 11). AI and digital technology can make the Measure and Analyze stages cheaper and quicker (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 264).

Criteria 4 and 5: 5 each. DMAIC works to eliminate excess material (Campoverde-Pillajo et al., 2026, p. 1) and wasted work hours, which can also be better for the environment.

Kaizen

Kaizen is a Japanese methodology of continuous improvement (Sahmi & El Abbadi, 2024, p. 169). Its name combines Kai and Zen, meaning change and good. Instead of being data-driven, it lets every worker make small improvements daily, resting on housekeeping, waste elimination, and standardization (Sichinsambwe et al., 2023, p. 3).

Criterion 1: 10. A survey of manufacturers linked Kaizen to a 50-70% reduction in lead time, a 50-100% increase in productivity, and 20-40% savings in production costs (Sichinsambwe et al., 2023, p. 6).

Criterion 2: 7. Individuals can make small differences often, so Kaizen adapts well (Kazancoglu et al., 2024, p. 1714). Relying less on data can make its solutions less accurate.

Criterion 3: 5. Kaizen depends on workforce competency and willingness to change (Sahmi & El Abbadi, 2024, p. 173). With engaged, well-educated workers it can be cheaper and quicker, but it may need extensive training (Sichinsambwe et al., 2023, p. 6), and people can be less reliable than pure data.

Criteria 4 and 5: 5 each. Waste elimination is a foundational step of Kaizen (Sichinsambwe et al., 2023, p. 3).

The verdict

Lean Six Sigma finished with 35 points to Kaizen’s 32, making it the better option. The two tie on efficiency, waste, and environmental impact. Kaizen wins on adaptability, but Lean Six Sigma pulls ahead on cost, because its data-driven results held up against the money spent. Choosing it means maintaining improvement over time through DMAIC, which should decrease lead time, improve profits, and drive efficiency.

What it takes: a disciplined workforce and a willingness to put funds into completing DMAIC, mainly the Analyze, Improve, and Control stages. In return, the long-term profit and consistency make the investment worthwhile.

Future of Lean Six Sigma

Lean Six Sigma was built over many decades to help manufacturing and management keep up with a changing world, and that change is now speeding up. Artificial intelligence has revolutionized everyday life, and it has already had a large impact on manufacturing. We are in the Fourth Industrial Revolution (I4), a time when people and organizations are connected all the time through digitization. As a result, AI and digital tools will help build products faster, with higher quality and fewer errors, in optimized manufacturing spaces (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 264).

Because of this, Lean Six Sigma will matter more, not less. New technology needs direction, and Lean Six Sigma finds the bottlenecks, improvement areas, and waste that show where AI and digital tools belong. In other words, those tools improve current processes rather than replace them, and Lean Six Sigma shows where they work best (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 275). That makes it the framework for bringing new technology into manufacturing.

Where AI fits into DMAIC

  • Measure. Inspection can be automated with machine learning, instead of relying only on sampled batches.
  • Measure and Analyze. With AI and digital technology, these stages can be cheaper and take less time to complete (Tissir et al., 2023, p. 264). That helps with the biggest weakness of DMAIC, its time cost.
  • Improve. AI can predict machine downtime better (George et al., 2019, Ch. 2, p. 26) and reduce setup time (George et al., 2019, Ch. 2, p. 30).

Once the right places are identified, the benefits become concrete, and the human framework keeps them pointed at real problems.

What stays the same

Data-based decisions, a focus on the customer’s needs, and the DMAIC cycle of measuring, changing, and checking. These are what keep new tools from being used for their own sake.

What changes

The speed and cost of measuring and analyzing, and the range of improvements available, such as better downtime prediction and shorter setup times.

Taken together, these points show why Lean Six Sigma is one of the most effective manufacturing methodologies to date. It has been shown to work across global markets, and as the world keeps changing it will let manufacturing adapt and expand, pushing the limits of technology like never before.
